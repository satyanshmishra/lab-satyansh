Materials that are attracted towards a magnet are magnetic. Those that are not attracted are non-magnetic.

Where is the magnet strongest?

Iron filings are spread on a sheet of paper over a bar magnet. Tap the paper to let them settle. Drag the magnet to move it.

What do you notice about where the filings collect?

Poles

Most filings stick near the two ends of the bar magnet. These ends are its poles: the North pole and the South pole. Every magnet has two poles.

A freely suspended magnet

A bar magnet hangs from a thread so it can turn freely. Drag it to spin it in any direction, let go, and see where it comes to rest. Do it three times.

The magnetic compass

A compass has a small magnetised needle that can rotate freely. Its north-pointing end is painted red. Once the needle points north, we can mark all the other directions.

Attract or repel?

Magnet A rests on round pencils so it can roll. Move magnet B closer and try both of its ends. You can also slide a wooden block between them.

Bring magnet B close to magnet A.

Like poles repel (N–N or S–S).
Unlike poles attract (N–S).
The magnet's force acts even through wood!

Magnes the shepherdA story says a shepherd named Magnes found that his iron-tipped stick stuck to a rock. That rock was magnetite, a natural magnet.
Look after magnetsKeep magnets away from mobiles, TVs and computers. Do not drop or hammer them; store bar magnets in pairs with unlike poles side by side.
Always two polesEven if you break a magnet, each piece still has a North and a South pole.
